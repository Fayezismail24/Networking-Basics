

### 1. What RX Power is

**RX Power** (Received Power) is the **amount of signal strength a device receives from a transmitter**.

* For example, in Wi-Fi, the router transmits a signal, and your laptop or phone measures how strong that signal is.
* In fiber optics or radio links, RX Power tells you how much light or radio signal actually arrives at the receiver.

**Unit:** Usually measured in **dBm** (decibels relative to 1 milliwatt).

---

### 2. What the numbers mean

* **High RX power** → strong signal, usually better connection and speed.
* **Low RX power** → weak signal, may cause slow speeds, disconnections, or packet loss.

**Typical wireless RX values:**

| RX Power (dBm) | Meaning                            |
| -------------- | ---------------------------------- |
| -30 to -50     | Excellent, very strong signal      |
| -50 to -60     | Good, reliable connection          |
| -60 to -70     | Fair, might experience slow speeds |
| -70 to -80     | Weak, may have disconnects         |
| < -80          | Very weak, likely unusable         |

> Note: In dBm, **closer to 0 is stronger**. So -40 dBm is stronger than -70 dBm.

---

### 3. Why RX power matters

1. **Signal quality:** Higher RX power usually means fewer errors in communication.
2. **Distance indicator:** If RX is weak, the device might be too far from the transmitter or something is blocking the signal.
3. **Troubleshooting:** Engineers check RX power to see if problems are due to weak signals or interference.

---

### 4. Factors that affect RX power

* **Distance**: Farther devices get weaker signals.
* **Obstacles**: Walls, furniture, and buildings reduce power.
* **Interference**: Other Wi-Fi networks, microwaves, or radio devices.
* **Transmit power**: If the transmitter isn’t strong enough, RX will naturally be low.

---

### 5. Quick example

* Router sends Wi-Fi at TX power -20 dBm.
* Your laptop shows RX power -55 dBm.

Interpretation: signal is **good**, not perfect, but stable.
